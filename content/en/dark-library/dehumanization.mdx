## The short version

Dehumanization is a family of ways of perceiving or representing people as less fully human. It can deny individuality, emotional depth, thought, self-control, civility, or moral standing. It may be blatant, as when a group is compared with pests or objects, or subtle, as when people are treated only as interchangeable units, bodies, cases, or obstacles.

This framing can make suffering less visible and some harmful choices easier to present as efficient, necessary, or inconsequential.

Dehumanization does **not** automatically produce violence, and violence does not require perpetrators to see targets as nonhuman. People may harm others precisely because they regard them as human agents who deserve punishment. Dehumanization is one possible process in a larger system of power, incentives, identity, fear, and moral justification.

## Why it matters

How people are represented affects which questions enter a decision. If those affected appear only as a burden, threat, inventory, or mass, their pain, intentions, relationships, rights, and testimony are easier to omit. The harm has not disappeared; its description has been narrowed.

Dehumanization can support moral exclusion—the sense that ordinary duties of care or fairness do not apply equally to a target. It can also enable instrumental harm, in which people are treated as means, costs, or barriers to another goal. Bureaucratic systems need categories and numbers, but aggregation becomes dangerous when there is no route back to individual circumstances and accountable judgment.

The causal direction may run both ways. A person or institution may dehumanize before imposing harm, or may adopt dehumanizing descriptions afterward to reduce discomfort and defend what was done. Identifying the language is therefore a warning and an invitation to inspect conduct, not proof of a predetermined outcome.

## How it works

Haslam's [integrative review](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr1003_4) proposed two broad forms. **Animalistic dehumanization** denies qualities treated as uniquely human, such as refinement, moral sensibility, or rationality, making others seem uncivilized or animal-like. **Mechanistic dehumanization** denies qualities associated with human nature, such as warmth, emotion, flexibility, or individuality, making others seem object-like or machine-like.

These are theoretical patterns, not a test that reveals what someone literally believes. Several processes can contribute:

- **Category compression:** diverse people are reduced to a single label and assumed to share one essence.
- **Mind denial:** targets are described without intentions, pain, memory, relationships, or a point of view.
- **Metaphor and labeling:** language of infestation, contamination, machinery, cargo, or waste changes which responses feel fitting.
- **Moral distance:** consequences for the target are excluded from the same standards applied to insiders.
- **Administrative abstraction:** a metric becomes the whole person, and what the system does not measure becomes easy to disregard.

Dehumanization overlaps with prejudice, dislike, stereotyping, objectification, and moral disengagement, but the terms are not identical. In a series of studies, Kteily and colleagues developed a direct measure of blatant dehumanization and found that it explained some hostile intergroup attitudes beyond related measures ([the research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121523/)). The samples and measurement choices limit how broadly any numerical result should be generalized.

## A concrete example

Imagine a fictional agency planning relocation after a flood. Internal documents refer to residents only as “noncompliant units” and “clearance load.” A dashboard records how quickly an area is emptied but not whether families understand the process, remain together, retain medicines, or have somewhere safe to go.

Numbers and logistics are necessary during an emergency. The dehumanizing shift occurs when throughput becomes the only relevant reality and residents' minds, rights, and circumstances disappear from the decision. A harmful relocation can then be framed as an operational success.

Restoring names, testimony, appeal routes, and measures of human impact does not decide every difficult allocation. It changes what the decision must account for. It also makes responsibility harder to hide behind a category.

Across five experiments, dehumanization was associated with or increased support for instrumental harm, but not harm participants considered morally deserved. One experiment also found the reverse: imagining instrumental harm increased dehumanization ([Rai and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5559031/)). Hypothetical tasks do not reproduce institutions or mass violence, but they show that motive and causal direction matter.

## Common misconceptions

- **“Any animal metaphor is dehumanization.”** Metaphors depend on context; affectionate, admiring, comic, and degrading comparisons do not function identically.
- **“Dehumanization is always explicit hate speech.”** It can also appear as cold objectification, erased individuality, or procedures that recognize only output.
- **“Once people are dehumanized, violence inevitably follows.”** Dehumanization is neither a sufficient nor necessary cause of violence.
- **“Violence proves the perpetrator did not see the victim as human.”** Some harm is moralized around blame and assumes the target can understand and suffer punishment.
- **“Numbers are inherently dehumanizing.”** Aggregated data can reveal neglected harm. The risk arises when metrics replace rather than complement human context.
- **“Calling perpetrators monsters prevents abuse.”** It may express condemnation, but it can obscure the human decisions, institutions, and incentives that require accountability.

## How to recognize it

Notice recurring descriptions that turn people into vermin, disease, objects, cargo, waste, machines, or a uniform mass. Look beyond vocabulary: Are targets granted individual motives and testimony? Is their pain treated as real? Can exceptions exist, or does group membership explain everything? Are protections applied symmetrically?

Examine institutional documents and dashboards for what is missing. Efficiency, risk, cost, and volume may be legitimate measures, but who bears the cost? Is there a way to contest errors? Are people able to speak for themselves, or are they discussed only through an authority's categories?

A harsh word does not establish a psychological state or causal chain. Repeated language aligned with exclusionary rules, concealed harm, collective blame, and lack of accountability is more concerning.

## How to protect yourself

Restore specificity without demanding exceptional worth. Distinguish individuals from categories, include their stated perspectives, and describe concrete consequences. Ask what mental states, relationships, rights, or alternatives the frame excludes.

Challenge collective claims with disaggregated evidence and consistent standards. Accountability does not require denying anyone's humanity: conduct can be condemned, constrained, or punished while individual evidence and basic rights remain intact.

Avoid repeatedly amplifying degrading labels merely to criticize them; repetition can normalize the frame and expose targets to further harm. When safe, document the original context, support affected people and independent witnesses, and direct attention toward policies, incentives, and decision-makers rather than treating hateful language as the entire mechanism.

In organizations, pair performance metrics with human-impact measures, appeal paths, and independent review. Humanization helps keep efficiency claims answerable to the people who bear their consequences.

## What remains uncertain

Researchers disagree about how dehumanization should be defined and measured. Trait ratings, mind-attribution tasks, metaphors, explicit scales, and archival language may capture different phenomena. Findings from one conflict or cultural setting may not transfer cleanly to another.

Over's [critical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32348704/) argues that prominent versions of the dehumanization hypothesis face unresolved conceptual and evidential challenges, including whether harmful actors truly deny humanity and whether dehumanization causes harm rather than follows it. Other research finds meaningful associations and experimental effects under particular conditions.

The cautious conclusion is that denying mind, individuality, or equal standing can change how harm is perceived and justified. It should be treated as a risk process to investigate—not a universal explanation, a diagnosis from one word, or an automatic path to violence.
