## The short version

Groupthink is a proposed pattern in which pressure for agreement and loyalty weakens a group's search for alternatives, criticism, and risk evaluation. Members may censor doubts, infer unanimity from silence, discount outside warnings, or protect a preferred plan from scrutiny.

It is a useful warning label, but not a settled law of group behavior. Research supports several component risks—conformity pressure, directive leadership, poor information sharing, and suppressed dissent—more strongly than it supports every link in the original groupthink model. That is why this page uses evidence level C.

## Why it matters

Groups make decisions that no individual can make alone. They combine expertise, distribute work, and catch errors. But collaboration only adds value when relevant information enters the discussion and can change the result.

An apparently unanimous decision may hide private doubt. If each person waits for someone else to object, silence becomes social evidence. A leader's early preference can further narrow discussion. Once identity and morality attach to the plan—“people like us support this”—criticism may feel like disloyalty rather than quality control.

The practical danger is not merely choosing a bad outcome. A group can reach a good outcome through a fragile process, or a bad outcome despite careful reasoning and bad luck. Decision quality should therefore be evaluated partly by whether the group seriously tested alternatives with the information available at the time.

## How it works

The classic model connects several conditions: a cohesive group, insulation from outside views, stress, structural faults, and leadership that favors a solution. Proposed symptoms include self-censorship, pressure on dissenters, rationalization, an illusion of unanimity, and confidence that the group is morally or practically invulnerable.

These conditions do not form an inevitable chain. In a historical comparison of ten decision episodes, Tetlock and colleagues found support for some classifications and process differences but less support for the complete causal model. Structural and procedural faults were more predictive than cohesion or stress alone; read the [study's measured test of the model](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.63.3.403).

Other mechanisms can create similar failures: confirmation bias, shared-information bias, group polarization, incentives, diffusion of responsibility, obedience, or simple lack of expertise. Saying “groupthink” should begin analysis, not end it.

## A concrete example

A hospital committee must decide whether to adopt new scheduling software. The chair negotiated the proposal and opens by calling it the only realistic option. Members received the vendor summary but not the incident reports from another hospital. A junior nurse knows the workflow creates a medication handoff risk, yet senior clinicians have already praised the plan.

The nurse stays silent. Two others privately interpret the silence as confirmation that frontline staff see no problem. The committee approves the system without comparing alternatives or running a simulation.

If the system later fails, it would be tempting to diagnose groupthink from the outcome alone. A more disciplined assessment asks what happened before the decision: Was contrary information available? Could it be voiced? Were alternatives specified? Did anyone test assumptions? These observations point to correctable process failures even if the broad label remains uncertain.

## Common misconceptions

- **“Any consensus is groupthink.”** Independent experts can converge because evidence is strong. Consensus is not itself a defect.
- **“Cohesive teams make worse decisions.”** Cohesion can support trust and candid disagreement. Evidence does not show a simple cohesion-to-failure rule.
- **“Only unintelligent groups are vulnerable.”** Expertise helps only if it is relevant, distributed, voiced, and used.
- **“A bad outcome proves a bad process.”** Luck and uncertainty matter. Outcome bias can make past warning signs seem more obvious than they were.
- **“Appointing a devil's advocate solves it.”** A ritual objection that carries no information or influence can become theater.

## How to recognize it

Look for repeated process signals: leaders announce conclusions before inquiry; alternatives are not recorded; objections are reframed as disloyalty; outside evidence is dismissed by source alone; apparent unanimity is inferred without an independent vote; and meetings discuss reasons the preferred plan will work but not conditions under which it would fail.

Also notice asymmetry after the meeting. If concerns appear in private conversations but not in the room where decisions are made, the formal consensus is unreliable.

## What to do with this

Collect initial judgments independently. Have leaders state goals and constraints before stating preferences. Assign different members to develop credible alternatives, invite relevant outsiders, and require a pre-mortem: “Imagine this failed—what likely caused it?” Record uncertainties and decision criteria before the outcome is known.

Separate idea criticism from loyalty and give dissent a real path to affect the decision. For high-stakes choices, schedule a second review after members have had time to seek disconfirming evidence. These practices improve information flow; they cannot guarantee a correct result.

## What remains uncertain

Reviews reach sharply different conclusions about groupthink's scientific status. Esser judged the research to have continuing heuristic value while noting that many theoretical ideas remained untested; see his [25-year review](https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.1998.2758). Fuller and Aldag argued that empirical support was remarkably limited and that the model had been presented too confidently; see their [critical assessment](https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.1998.2760). Broader research confirms that groups can show both process gains and losses depending on information structure, motivation, and procedure, as summarized by [Kerr and Tindale](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.142009). The safest conclusion is specific: design groups so dissent and unique information can be expressed, without assuming one label explains every failure.
