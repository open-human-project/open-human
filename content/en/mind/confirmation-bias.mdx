## The short version

Confirmation bias is a family of tendencies to seek, interpret, remember, or test information in ways that favor a belief already under consideration. It does not mean people deliberately ignore reality. Often, the same evidence simply receives different scrutiny depending on whether it supports or threatens what someone expects.

The bias is well established, but it is not an all-purpose explanation for disagreement. Prior beliefs can sometimes be reasonable, and changing your mind after every new claim would be poor reasoning too. The useful question is whether you apply comparable standards to evidence on both sides.

## Why it matters

Beliefs guide decisions: whom to trust, which treatment question to ask a clinician, whether a business idea is working, or how to interpret another person's behavior. If supporting cases feel vivid while counterexamples are explained away, confidence can increase without accuracy increasing.

Confirmation bias can also become social. Search results, friends, media choices, and group norms influence which evidence reaches you in the first place. Two people can then inhabit different information samples and each feel that the facts are obvious. Raymond Nickerson's broad [review of the evidence](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175) describes confirmation bias across hypothesis testing, memory, science, policy, and everyday reasoning.

## How it works

The process can enter at several points:

- **Search:** You ask, “Why is this true?” rather than, “What would distinguish true from false?”
- **Attention:** Supporting details stand out while conflicting details receive less notice.
- **Evaluation:** Friendly evidence gets a quick pass; hostile evidence gets an intensive methods review.
- **Memory:** Consistent examples may be easier to retrieve later, making the belief feel even better supported.
- **Testing:** You choose tests that can confirm a hypothesis but have little chance of disproving it.

In Wason's classic [rule-discovery experiments](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470216008416717), participants often proposed examples consistent with their current rule rather than examples designed to separate it from alternatives. That pattern illustrates a central problem: many observations can fit more than one explanation. Evidence is most informative when competing explanations predict different outcomes.

Motivation can strengthen the bias, but motivation is not required. Limited time, ambiguous evidence, familiar mental models, and the ordinary need to reduce uncertainty can all contribute.

## A concrete example

Suppose a manager believes remote employees are less committed. One remote employee misses a deadline, and the manager treats it as revealing. An office employee misses one, and the manager explains it as an unusual workload problem. When remote work goes smoothly, it is considered normal and forgotten.

The observation itself is real; the asymmetry lies in how it is interpreted and remembered. A better test would define “commitment” before looking at the data, compare similar roles and workloads, include both successful and missed deadlines, and ask what result would count against the original belief.

A landmark experiment found a related pattern: people with opposing positions evaluated mixed studies about capital punishment in ways that favored their prior view, sometimes becoming more polarized. The details and limits of that result matter, but the [original study](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.11.2098) remains a clear demonstration of biased assimilation.

## Common misconceptions

- **“Only uninformed people do this.”** Expertise can improve judgment, but it can also provide more tools for defending a preferred conclusion.
- **“Any stable belief proves bias.”** A belief may persist because the evidence genuinely supports it. Bias concerns the process, not simply the conclusion.
- **“Looking at the other side fixes it.”** Weak counterarguments can create false reassurance. The goal is to engage with the strongest credible alternative.
- **“Confirmation bias and lying are the same.”** Most biased processing is not conscious deception.
- **“Both sides must be equally correct.”** Symmetrical evaluation does not require pretending unequal evidence is equal.

## What to do with this

Before searching, write down your claim and one observation that would meaningfully lower your confidence. Generate at least two plausible explanations, then ask what each predicts. Search directly for strong disconfirming evidence and evaluate it using the same standards you use for supporting evidence.

For consequential decisions, separate evidence collection from advocacy. Invite someone who does not share your incentives to review the reasoning. Use probabilities rather than “certain” or “impossible,” and record forecasts so hindsight cannot quietly rewrite what you expected. None of these steps guarantees neutrality; they make asymmetry easier to see.

## What remains uncertain

“Confirmation bias” covers several mechanisms, and their size varies with the task, incentives, expertise, emotional investment, and information environment. Some apparently confirmatory strategies can be efficient when prior probabilities are strong or when testing costs are unequal. Evidence on debiasing is also context-dependent: a technique that improves one laboratory task may not transfer broadly. The robust conclusion is not that humans always reject contrary facts, but that prior beliefs can systematically shape how evidence is sampled and judged.
