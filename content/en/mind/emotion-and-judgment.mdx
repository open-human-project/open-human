## The short version

Emotion is part of judgment, not simply noise added to otherwise pure reason. Feelings help mark what matters, direct attention, prepare action, and assign value. They can improve decisions by signaling needs and consequences. They can also bias a choice when their source is misunderstood or when an immediate impulse overwhelms longer-term goals.

Different emotions do not have one generic effect. Fear, anger, sadness, and joy can produce different patterns because they carry different appraisals of certainty, control, loss, and responsibility. A useful aim is not to become emotionless, but to understand which feeling is influencing which judgment—and whether that influence fits the present decision.

## Why it matters

Judgment underlies everyday questions: Is this person trustworthy? Is this risk acceptable? Should I send this message? What outcome is worth pursuing? Emotional responses can change what evidence receives attention, how probable an outcome feels, and how much weight is given to immediate versus delayed consequences.

A major [review of emotion and decision research](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115043) concludes that emotions are pervasive, sometimes beneficial and sometimes harmful influences on choice. Treating every emotional decision as irrational misses half the picture. Concern may highlight a genuine hazard; guilt may support repair; enthusiasm may sustain effort. Problems arise when a signal is inaccurate, disproportionate, or carried into an unrelated choice.

## How it works

Researchers often distinguish **integral emotion**, which arises from the decision itself, from **incidental emotion**, which comes from elsewhere. Anxiety about a dangerous road is integral to choosing a route. Irritation from an earlier argument is incidental to evaluating a colleague, even if it changes the evaluation.

Emotion can influence judgment through several pathways:

- It directs attention toward threats, rewards, injustice, or loss.
- It changes which memories and interpretations are easiest to access.
- It supplies a rapid feeling of attraction, aversion, urgency, or confidence.
- It carries appraisal patterns. Fear commonly involves uncertainty and low control; anger often involves greater certainty and perceived control.
- It changes action readiness—for example, withdrawal, confrontation, or repair.

The “feelings-as-information” account shows why source awareness matters. In a classic experiment, weather affected reported life satisfaction, but drawing attention to the weather reduced that relationship; see the [original study](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.45.3.513). A feeling can be real while its apparent explanation is mistaken.

## A concrete example

After a hostile meeting, you receive a short message: “Can we revisit the proposal tomorrow?” Residual anger makes the ambiguity feel disrespectful. You infer hostile intent, rate the sender as less trustworthy, and draft an aggressive reply.

Anger is not merely “negative mood.” Experiments by Lerner and Keltner found that [fear and anger produced different risk judgments](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.81.1.146): fear was associated with more pessimistic, risk-averse estimates, while anger was associated with more optimistic, risk-seeking ones. The result does not predict every individual's behavior, but it demonstrates why emotional valence alone—positive versus negative—is too simple.

Pausing may reveal two separate facts: the meeting genuinely crossed a boundary, and the new message is ambiguous. That separation allows the first problem to be addressed without using it as unexamined evidence about the second.

## Common misconceptions

- **“Emotion is the opposite of reason.”** Emotion and cognition interact throughout evaluation and choice.
- **“Calm decisions are unbiased.”** A calm person still has goals, learned values, assumptions, and incomplete information.
- **“Trust your gut” is always wise.** Intuition can reflect expertise, but also fear, habit, prejudice, or a misleading context.
- **“Never decide while emotional.”** Many decisions necessarily involve emotion; urgency and stakes determine whether delay is useful.
- **“All negative emotions make people cautious.”** Specific emotions can have opposing effects.

## What to do with this

Name the emotion precisely, then ask what caused it and what action it is preparing. Separate observations from interpretations: “The reply was two words” is different from “They dismissed me.” Ask whether the feeling is integral to this decision or carried over from another event.

When arousal is high and delay is safe, create time before irreversible action. For high-stakes choices, write down criteria, base rates, and alternatives before evaluating them; seek a second perspective that is not exposed to the same immediate emotion. Do not use this framework to invalidate another person's feelings. A feeling is genuine information about their state even when a proposed interpretation remains uncertain.

## What remains uncertain

Effects vary with culture, personality, regulation strategies, experience, context, and the intensity or combination of emotions. Laboratory inductions simplify feelings that are mixed and dynamic in real life. Researchers also disagree about the best theories and categories of emotion. Strong evidence supports the broad claim that emotion shapes judgment; predicting exactly how one emotion will affect one person in one decision remains much harder.
