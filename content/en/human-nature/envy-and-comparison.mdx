## The short version

Envy is a painful response to another person having an advantage we want for ourselves. The advantage might involve skill, recognition, money, appearance, freedom, a relationship, or an opportunity. It usually depends on a comparison that feels relevant: the other person is close enough, similar enough, or competing in a domain important to the self.

Envy is often confused with jealousy. A useful distinction is structural. **Envy usually concerns two parties and an advantage: I want what you have. Jealousy usually concerns three positions and a threatened bond: I fear that a rival may displace me in a relationship I value.** Both can occur together, and everyday language does not always keep the terms separate.

The emotion does not contain one compulsory action. Envy can focus attention on a gap and motivate learning. It can also prompt withdrawal, self-criticism, denial of another person's merit, or pleasure at their setback. Understanding the comparison creates a choice; it does not make every response harmless.

## Why it matters

Envy is socially uncomfortable, so people often hide it from others and themselves. It may appear in a safer disguise: “Their work is overrated,” “They were only lucky,” or “I never wanted that anyway.” Sometimes those claims are true. The difficulty is that envy can change which evidence feels convincing without announcing itself.

Parrott and Smith experimentally examined the two emotions and found different reported patterns: envy included inferiority, longing, and resentment, while jealousy included fear of loss, distrust, anxiety, and anger. Their research on [distinguishing envy and jealousy](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.64.6.906) does not imply perfectly separate categories in every language or episode. It provides a clearer question: am I reacting mainly to someone else's advantage, or to a perceived threat to a valued relationship?

Naming envy can also prevent a moral shortcut. Feeling it does not make someone a bad person, and denying it does not make behavior fair. Responsibility begins with what a person does with the feeling—what they investigate, communicate, practice, withhold, or damage.

## How it works

Envy becomes more likely when three appraisals converge. First, another person has something desirable. Second, the domain matters to the observer's goals or self-concept. Third, the comparison highlights a disadvantage that feels hard to close, unfair, or threatening to status. Smith and Kim's broad [review of envy research](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.46) describes inferiority, hostility, and resentment as common parts of the experience while emphasizing its links with comparison and self-evaluation.

Similarity can sharpen the contrast. A stranger winning an award in an unfamiliar field may have little emotional effect; a peer receiving the promotion you sought can make a nearby alternative life visible. Public rankings and carefully selected online displays multiply available comparisons, but exposure alone does not determine the response. Current mood, perceived control, closeness, deservingness, identity, and the importance of the domain can all matter.

Researchers have proposed two recurring patterns. In **benign envy**, attention is directed toward moving oneself upward: greater effort, learning, or pursuit of the advantage. In **malicious envy**, motivation is directed toward pulling the advantaged person down through derogation or harm. Van de Ven and colleagues reported distinct experiential patterns across studies in the Netherlands, United States, and Spain; see their work on [leveling up and leveling down](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19485619/). “Benign” and “malicious” describe motivational forms, not permanent types of people.

Later work by Lange and Crusius linked these patterns to different tendencies and appraisals in their study of [the motivational dynamics of dispositional envy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25534243/). The distinction remains debated: some researchers reserve the word envy for its hostile form, and translations do not always provide separate everyday labels. It is safest to describe the actual thought and action tendency rather than assume a label settles the mechanism.

Envy can also carry information about systems. A colleague's resentment may partly reflect an opaque promotion process or unequal access, not merely an individual inability to celebrate success. Psychological explanation should not convert real exclusion or unfairness into a personality problem.

## A concrete example

Sari and Maya began designing at the same company. Maya's project is featured publicly, and she is invited to present it to senior leaders. Sari feels a drop in her stomach, then rapidly notices flaws in Maya's work. She tells herself the recognition was political and avoids congratulating her.

Several claims are mixed together. The selection process may indeed favor visibility. Maya's work may also be strong. Sari wants recognition, believes they are comparable, and now sees herself as falling behind. None of these facts requires her to deny the emotion or accept its first interpretation.

Sari separates four questions: What exactly do I want? What did Maya do that I could learn? What evidence suggests the process was unfair? What action would I respect afterward? She congratulates Maya without pretending to feel uncomplicated joy, asks for feedback on her own portfolio, and requests that future selection criteria be made explicit. If unfairness exists, she can challenge the process with evidence rather than using Maya as its substitute.

## Common misconceptions

- **“Envy and jealousy are the same.”** They can overlap, but envy centers on another's advantage while jealousy centers on a threatened valued bond.
- **“Envy proves I secretly want the other person harmed.”** Hostility is possible, not inevitable. Envy can also lead to learning, distance, or no action.
- **“Successful people cause envy by sharing success.”** An emotional response emerges from a relationship among observer, target, domain, and context. It is not automatically the target's fault.
- **“Every criticism of a successful person is envy.”** Criticism may be accurate. Calling it envy can be a way to avoid examining evidence or power.
- **“Comparison is always harmful.”** Comparison can provide information and attainable models. Its usefulness depends on what is compared and what follows.
- **“Benign envy is always good.”** Improvement efforts can become compulsive, unsafe, or aimed at a status contest that does not deserve the cost.

## What to do with this

Name the desired advantage precisely: not “their life,” but a flexible schedule, recognized skill, stable income, or public credit. Then separate the observable fact from the story about worth. “They received the award” does not by itself mean “I am failing” or “the decision was corrupt.” Those are hypotheses requiring other evidence.

Ask whether the domain genuinely matters to you or became urgent only because it was displayed. If it matters, identify one controllable learning step and one structural question. You might seek feedback, study a skill, clarify criteria, or document unequal treatment. If the comparison supplies no usable information, reducing repeated exposure may protect attention without requiring contempt for the other person.

Do not use honesty about envy as permission to burden or punish its target. Pause before gossiping, sabotaging, or making a permanent judgment. If the emotion repeatedly drives harsh self-criticism, aggression, or major restriction in daily life, qualified support may help; an educational concept cannot assess an individual situation.

## What remains uncertain

Envy research relies heavily on recalled episodes, questionnaires, scenarios, and samples from a limited set of societies. People may underreport a stigmatized emotion, and languages divide the emotional landscape differently. The boundary between benign and malicious envy, and whether they are distinct forms or different consequences of one experience, remains contested.

The reliable conclusion is narrower: an upward comparison in a personally important domain can produce a painful emotion involving disadvantage and desire. That emotion can be associated with different motivations, but it neither reveals one fixed character nor dictates one action. Context, perceived control, fairness, and chosen response all shape what happens next.
